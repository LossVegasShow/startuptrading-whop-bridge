## 3. Full script (read once, then talk it in his own words)

**Target search:** how many contracts should I trade · **Pillar:** Risk & sizing · **Chapters:** 0:00 The backwards way · 1:10 The one number · 3:00 ATR, plain English · 5:00 ES example · 7:00 NQ and the micro · 9:00 When it says zero · 10:15 Recap

> *[Cold open: ES 5-minute chart, a stop line drawn 6 points below an entry.]*
>
> Most traders pick their size before they look at the chart. Two contracts because yesterday was one. Five because they feel good. Then they put the stop wherever it fits that size. That's backwards, and it's the reason most accounts I've seen didn't make it.
>
> By the end of this you'll have one formula, and you'll know exactly how many contracts to trade on any setup, in any market. Three steps: the one number you decide first, how ATR sets your stop, and the math that turns both into a size.
>
> *[Cut to Jack.]*
>
> **Step one. Decide the dollar risk before anything else.**
>
> This is the only number you choose. It's how much you're willing to lose if the trade is wrong. For a lot of people that's one percent of the account or less. On a $25,000 account, that's $250. Whatever it is, you set it before the market opens, and it doesn't change because a setup looks good. *[On screen: "Risk per trade = decided first".]*
>
> **Step two. Let the market set the stop.**
>
> Here's where people go wrong. They pick a stop in points because it sounds right. Four points on ES. Always four. But the market doesn't move the same amount every day. A quiet Tuesday and a CPI morning are completely different animals.
>
> *[Chart: ATR indicator added under price.]*
>
> ATR, average true range, is just how far the market has been moving per bar, on average. On this 5-minute ES chart, ATR is reading about 4 points. I put my stop about one and a half ATRs away. So that's 6 points. On a busier day ATR might be 8, and the stop goes to 12. The stop breathes with the market. That's the whole idea.
>
> **Step three. Let the stop decide the size.**
>
> Now the math. Every futures contract has a dollar value per point. ES is $50 a point. So a 6-point stop on one ES contract risks 6 times 50, which is $300.
>
> *[On screen: Contracts = risk ÷ (stop in points × $ per point).]*
>
> Contracts equals your risk, divided by the stop in points times the dollars per point. Round down. Always down.
>
> Say my risk is $500. $500 divided by $300 is 1.66. Round down, and that's 1 contract. My real risk on this trade is $300, under my limit. Notice I didn't choose "one contract". The market did.
>
> *[Chart: NQ, same day.]*
>
> Same thing on NQ. NQ is $20 a point, but it moves a lot more. ATR is reading about 20 points here, so one and a half ATRs is a 30-point stop. 30 times 20 is $600 for one contract. My risk is $500, and $500 divided by $600 rounds down to zero.
>
> **When the math says zero.**
>
> This is the most important part of the video. When the math says zero, it's telling you something. One full contract is too much for your account on this setup today. You have two honest choices: skip the trade, or go to the micro.
>
> MNQ is the micro Nasdaq. It's a tenth of the size, $2 a point. So the same 30-point stop is $60 a contract. $500 divided by $60 is 8.33, which rounds down to 8 micros, for $480 of real risk. Same idea, same stop, and the size finally fits.
>
> What you don't do is take one NQ anyway and pull the stop in to 20 points so it "fits". That's the trade that gets stopped by noise, then runs without you.
>
> *[Cut to Jack.]*
>
> **Recap.** Decide the dollar risk first. Let ATR set the stop. Let the stop set the size, and round down. If it says zero, skip it or go micro.
>
> I put a free calculator on the website that does this for 14 contracts, the link's in the description. And on NinjaTrader my free ATR indicator puts the number right on the chart. I'm live every weekday at nine Eastern if you want to watch me do this on real trades, including the ones that don't work.
>
